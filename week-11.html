Mass Storage: Disks, SSDs, Scheduling and RAID

How hard disks and flash drives actually store and retrieve data, why seek and rotation dominate disk performance, how the OS orders requests with FCFS, SSTF, SCAN and LOOK, and how RAID combines drives for speed and fault tolerance.

  • Estimate rebuild time and the risk of an unrecoverable read error during a RAID 5 rebuild

In Week 10 page replacement kept asking one thing of the disk: fetch this page, write that one back. We treated the disk as a black box with a fixed service time. This week we open the box. Secondary storage is the slowest and largest tier of the memory hierarchy, and the physics of how it works shapes the design of everything built on it: the paging policies of the last two chapters, and the file systems of Week 12 and Week 13.

We look at the two technologies in use today. Magnetic hard disk drives (HDDs) have moving parts, so their performance is a story about mechanical delays, and the OS can help by ordering requests sensibly. Solid-state drives (SSDs) have no moving parts but have their own awkward rules about erasing, and hide them behind a layer of firmware. Finally we combine several drives into a RAID array, trading capacity for speed and reliability. Arithmetic matters a lot this week, so every example is worked with real numbers.

HDD geometry and access time

A hard disk is a stack of rigid circular platters coated with magnetic material, spinning together on one spindle. Each platter surface has its own read/write head on an arm; all arms move together on one actuator. Data are recorded in concentric rings called tracks. The set of tracks at the same arm position on all surfaces is a cylinder. Each track is divided into sectors, the smallest unit the drive reads or writes: traditionally 512 bytes, and 4096 bytes on modern "Advanced Format" drives.

Old systems addressed a sector by (cylinder, head, sector). Modern drives present a flat array of numbered blocks, logical block addressing (LBA): block 0, 1, 2, and so on. The firmware maps LBAs to the physical layout, remaps bad sectors to spares, and hides the zones in which outer tracks hold more sectors than inner ones (zoned bit recording). Consecutive LBAs are usually physically close, which is the only fact the OS relies on.

The three delays

To read a block the drive must perform three steps, each taking time.

  1. Seek. Move the arm to the right track. Roughly 0.5 to 1 ms to the adjacent track, 4 to 10 ms on average for a random seek, up to 15 to 20 ms across the whole surface.
  2. Rotational latency. Wait for the wanted sector to rotate under the head. On average this is half a rotation.
  3. Transfer. Read the bytes as the sector passes under the head, then send them over the bus.

Disk access time

access time = seek time + rotational latency + transfer time (plus a small controller overhead). The average rotational latency is half of one rotation: (60 / RPM) / 2 seconds. The transfer time of b bytes is b / transfer rate.

A 7200 RPM disk turns once in 60 / 7200 = 8.33 ms, so its average rotational latency is 4.17 ms. Spindle speeds of 5400, 7200, 10,000 and 15,000 RPM give half-rotation delays of 5.56, 4.17, 3.00 and 2.00 ms. The program below computes the cost of a random 4 KiB read for several disks, and of a random 1 MiB read, from these formulas.

#include <stdio.h>

/* Average time (ms) for one random I/O on a hard disk. */
static void report(const char *name, double rpm, double seek_ms,
                   double mb_per_s, double bytes) {
    double rot_ms      = 60000.0 / rpm;                 /* one full rotation */
    double latency_ms  = rot_ms / 2.0;                  /* average: half a turn */
    double transfer_ms = bytes / (mb_per_s * 1e6) * 1000.0;
    double total_ms    = seek_ms + latency_ms + transfer_ms;
    double iops        = 1000.0 / total_ms;
    printf("%-12s rot %5.2f ms  seek %5.2f  latency %5.2f  xfer %6.3f  total %6.3f ms"
           "  %6.1f IOPS  %5.2f MB/s\n",
           name, rot_ms, seek_ms, latency_ms, transfer_ms, total_ms, iops,
           bytes * iops / 1e6);
}

int main(void) {
    printf("Random 4 KiB reads\n");
    report("5400 rpm",  5400,  12.0, 100.0, 4096);
    report("7200 rpm",  7200,   9.0, 150.0, 4096);
    report("15000 rpm", 15000,  4.0, 200.0, 4096);
    printf("Random 1 MiB reads on the 7200 rpm disk\n");
    report("7200 rpm",  7200,   9.0, 150.0, 1048576);
    printf("Sequential streaming on the 7200 rpm disk: %.0f MB/s (no seeks)\n", 150.0);
    return 0;
}

Study these numbers, because they explain most of the chapter. A random 4 KiB read on a 7200 RPM disk takes 13.2 ms, of which the actual data transfer is 0.027 ms: over 99.8 percent of the time is mechanical positioning. The disk delivers 76 operations per second, 0.31 MB/s, though the same drive can stream 150 MB/s when reading sequentially. Sequential access is roughly 500 times faster than random access on the same hardware. Reading 1 MiB at a time amortizes the seek and gets 52 MB/s. This is why file systems try hard to keep a file's blocks contiguous, why the OS reads ahead, and why scheduling the order of requests is worth the effort.

A record player with a slow arm

Picture a vinyl record. Playing a song straight through is fast. Jumping between random tracks means lifting the arm, moving it, and waiting for the record to spin to the right spot. The music itself takes microseconds; the arm and the spinning take milliseconds. A disk is that record player, just with a much smaller arm and a much faster spin.

Solid-state drives

An SSD stores bits in NAND flash memory: transistors that trap electric charge. There are no moving parts, so a random read costs tens of microseconds regardless of address, a thousand times faster than a disk seek. Flash comes with three rules that shape everything.

  • Reads and writes are by page, erasure is by block. A page is typically 4 to 16 KiB. An erase block is a group of pages (commonly 128 to 512, so 1 to 8 MiB).
  • A page can be programmed (written) only if it is erased, and the only way to erase is to wipe a whole block. A flash page cannot be overwritten in place.
  • Blocks wear out. Each erase damages the cells. A block survives a limited number of program/erase cycles, roughly 100,000 for single-level cell (SLC), around 10,000 for MLC, 3,000 for TLC and 1,000 for QLC (rough figures that vary by product).

Typical times are on the order of 25 to 100 µs for a page read, a few hundred microseconds for a page program, and several milliseconds for a block erase. Writes are far slower than reads and erases are slower still.

The flash translation layer

Rule 2 means a naive "overwrite sector 7" would require copying the whole block elsewhere. SSDs hide this behind firmware, the flash translation layer (FTL), which presents the same block interface as a disk.

The FTL does four jobs.

  • Address mapping. A table maps each logical block to a physical (block, page). Writes go to the next free page of an open block, like a log.
  • Garbage collection. When free blocks run low, pick a block with many stale pages, copy its remaining valid pages to a new block, and erase it. Copying costs extra writes.
  • Wear leveling. Spread erases evenly so no block dies early. Dynamic wear leveling directs new writes to young blocks; static wear leveling also relocates rarely changed (cold) data off young blocks so that they can take part in the rotation.
  • Bad block management and ECC. Retire failed blocks and correct bit errors; flash raw bit error rates rise with wear.

The OS helps with a command called TRIM (SATA) or deallocate (NVMe), which tells the drive which logical blocks the file system has freed. Without it the FTL would treat deleted data as valid and copy it needlessly. Linux issues it via fstrim or the discard mount option.

Write amplification and over-provisioning

Write amplification

Write amplification (WA) is the ratio of data written to flash to data written by the host: WA = flash writes / host writes. WA of 1 is ideal. Garbage collection copies push it above 1.

When GC must reclaim a block, the more valid pages it contains, the more it must copy. A drive with spare room (over-provisioning: flash capacity that the host cannot see, often 7 to 28 percent) can pick blocks that are mostly stale, so WA falls. The program below is a toy FTL: a chip of 16 blocks of 8 pages (128 pages), greedy garbage collection, and a host that fills the drive and then does 20,000 random overwrites. It repeats this for several levels of exposed capacity.

#include <stdio.h>
#include <stdlib.h>

#define BLOCKS 16              /* erase blocks on the "chip" */
#define PPB    8               /* pages per block */
#define PHYS   (BLOCKS * PPB)  /* 128 physical pages */

static unsigned long long seed = 99;
static int rnd(int m) {
    seed = seed * 6364136223846793005ULL + 1442695040888963407ULL;
    return (int)((seed >> 33) % m);
}

static int map[PHYS];          /* logical page -> physical page, -1 = unmapped */
static int owner[PHYS];        /* physical page -> logical page */
static int state[PHYS];        /* 0 erased, 1 valid, 2 invalid (stale) */
static int erases[BLOCKS];
static int cur = 0, curpage = 0;   /* block being filled (-1 = none), next page in it */
static long host_writes, flash_writes;

static int valid_in(int b) {
    int c = 0;
    for (int i = 0; i < PPB; i++) c += state[b * PPB + i] == 1;
    return c;
}

static int block_is_erased(int b) {
    for (int i = 0; i < PPB; i++) if (state[b * PPB + i] != 0) return 0;
    return 1;
}

static void program(int lpn) {          /* flash pages are written once, in order */
    int p = cur * PPB + curpage;
    if (map[lpn] >= 0) state[map[lpn]] = 2;           /* the old copy becomes stale */
    state[p] = 1; owner[p] = lpn; map[lpn] = p; flash_writes++;
    if (++curpage == PPB) cur = -1;
}

/* Greedy garbage collection: pick the full block with the fewest valid pages,
   erase it, and write its surviving pages back into it. */
static void gc(void) {
    int victim = -1;
    for (int b = 0; b < BLOCKS; b++)
        if (victim < 0 || valid_in(b) < valid_in(victim)) victim = b;
    int moved[PPB], m = 0;
    for (int i = 0; i < PPB; i++)
        if (state[victim * PPB + i] == 1) moved[m++] = owner[victim * PPB + i];
    for (int i = 0; i < PPB; i++) state[victim * PPB + i] = 0;
    erases[victim]++;
    cur = victim; curpage = 0;
    for (int i = 0; i < m; i++) { map[moved[i]] = -1; program(moved[i]); }
}

static void host_write(int lpn) {
    host_writes++;
    if (cur < 0) {
        for (int b = 0; b < BLOCKS && cur < 0; b++)
            if (block_is_erased(b)) { cur = b; curpage = 0; }
        if (cur < 0) gc();
    }
    program(lpn);
}

int main(void) {
    int exposed[] = {50, 70, 80, 90};          /* percent of physical space shown to the host */
    printf("%d physical pages in %d erase blocks of %d pages\n", PHYS, BLOCKS, PPB);
    printf("exposed  spare  host writes  flash writes  write amp.  erases max/min\n");
    for (int f = 0; f < 4; f++) {
        int logical = PHYS * exposed[f] / 100;
        for (int i = 0; i < PHYS; i++) { map[i] = -1; owner[i] = -1; state[i] = 0; }
        for (int b = 0; b < BLOCKS; b++) erases[b] = 0;
        cur = 0; curpage = 0; seed = 99;
        for (int l = 0; l < logical; l++) host_write(l);               /* fill the drive */
        host_writes = flash_writes = 0;
        for (int i = 0; i < 20000; i++) host_write(rnd(logical));     /* random overwrites */
        int mx = 0, mn = 1 << 30;
        for (int b = 0; b < BLOCKS; b++) {
            if (erases[b] > mx) mx = erases[b];
            if (erases[b] < mn) mn = erases[b];
        }
        printf("%6d%%  %4d%%  %11ld  %12ld  %10.2f  %6d/%d\n", exposed[f],
               100 - exposed[f], host_writes, flash_writes,
               (double)flash_writes / host_writes, mx, mn);
    }
    return 0;
}

With half the chip held back, each host write costs 1.18 flash writes; with only 10 percent spare, 3.63. The cost of GC rises steeply as the drive fills up, which is why SSDs slow down and wear faster when nearly full, and why you can extend a drive's life by leaving part of it unpartitioned or trimmed. The erase counts at the end of each run also show wear: the busiest block was erased 657 times and the least busy 433 in the last row; a real FTL works to narrow that gap.

SSDs attach by SATA (a single command queue of 32 entries, designed for disks) or by NVMe over PCIe, which supports up to 65,535 queues of up to 65,535 commands, so many CPU cores can issue I/O in parallel without sharing a lock.

Disk scheduling

Many processes issue disk requests, so a queue of pending requests builds up. In what order should the drive serve them? Because seek time dominates, a good order cuts the total head movement. The OS (and the drive's own firmware, which also reorders) wants to improve two things: throughput (requests served per second) and fairness (no request waits forever).

Our test case is the classic one. The disk has cylinders 0 to 199, the head is at cylinder 53, and the queue holds requests for cylinders 98, 183, 37, 122, 14, 124, 65, 67 (in arrival order). The measure is the total number of cylinders the head crosses.

FCFS

First come, first served serves the requests in arrival order: 53 → 98 → 183 → 37 → 122 → 14 → 124 → 65 → 67. The moves are 45, 85, 146, 85, 108, 110, 59 and 2, a total of 640 cylinders. It is fair and trivially simple, but the head flies back and forth.

SSTF

Shortest seek time first always serves the request closest to the head: 53 → 65 → 67 → 37 → 14 → 98 → 122 → 124 → 183. The moves are 12, 2, 30, 23, 84, 24, 2 and 59, a total of 236. Much better, but SSTF can starve requests: if new requests keep arriving near the head, a distant request waits indefinitely. It is a form of shortest-job-first and inherits SJF's problem.

SCAN (the elevator algorithm)

SCAN moves the head in one direction, serving requests on the way, until it reaches the end of the disk, then reverses. Like an elevator, it sweeps up and down. Toward cylinder 0 first: 53 → 37 → 14 → 0 → 65 → 67 → 98 → 122 → 124 → 183, moves 16, 23, 14, 65, 2, 31, 24, 2 and 59, a total of 236. If the head is moving toward 199 first: 53 → 65 → 67 → 98 → 122 → 124 → 183 → 199 → 37 → 14, moves 12, 2, 31, 24, 2, 59, 16, 162 and 23, a total of 331. The direction matters: here going toward 0 first is cheaper, because only two requests lie below the head, and it so happens to tie with SSTF.

SCAN avoids starvation because every request is served within one round trip. It still favours the middle cylinders, which the head passes twice in a short time, while a request just behind the head must wait nearly two full sweeps.

C-SCAN

Circular SCAN sweeps in one direction only. On reaching the last cylinder it jumps back to cylinder 0 without serving anything and starts again. Toward 199: 53 → 199 (146), return jump to 0 (199), then 0 → 14 → 37 (14 and 23), a total of 382 when the return jump is counted. The jump is a fast, single long seek, and some textbooks leave it out, which would give 183; the choice must always be stated. The benefit is a more uniform waiting time, since each cylinder is visited once per cycle.

LOOK and C-LOOK

SCAN and C-SCAN go to the physical end of the disk even when no request is there. LOOK reverses at the last request in the current direction, and C-LOOK jumps from the last request to the first request at the other end. Toward 199: LOOK is 53 → 183 (130) and then 183 → 14 (169), a total of 299. C-LOOK is 53 → 183 (130), jump 183 → 14 (169), then 14 → 37 (23), a total of 322.

Scheduling is not the whole story

These algorithms minimize seek distance and ignore rotation, and a modern drive hides its geometry, so the OS cannot know true cylinder numbers. Today the OS sorts and merges by LBA, and the drive's firmware (with NCQ, up to 32 outstanding commands on SATA) picks the final order using its knowledge of the real head position. On an SSD there is no seek, so reordering by address brings little: Linux uses simple schedulers (none for NVMe, mq-deadline or bfq for others) that mainly merge adjacent requests and bound latency. We return to kernel I/O schedulers in Week 13.

How do you choose? SSTF or LOOK-style policies suit a lightly loaded disk. Under heavy load, SCAN and C-SCAN variants perform best and prevent starvation. For file systems that allocate blocks well, requests often fall in one area, and the choice matters less than the allocation policy. Real kernels also add deadlines so that a request cannot wait longer than a few hundred milliseconds.

RAID

A single drive is limited in speed and eventually fails. Combining several drives into a RAID (Redundant Array of Independent Disks, originally "Inexpensive") addresses both: spread data across drives for parallel speed, and store redundant information to survive failures. The OS or a controller presents the array as one big disk. Two basic ideas are used in combination.

  • Striping splits data into fixed-size stripe units (chunks, for example 64 KiB) placed round-robin across disks. Large reads and writes run on all disks in parallel.
  • Redundancy is either a mirror (a full second copy) or parity, the XOR of the data blocks, which allows a lost block to be recomputed.

The levels

RAID 0 stripes with no redundancy: all the capacity, full speed, but if any one disk fails the whole array's data is lost (the failure probability grows with the number of disks). RAID 1 mirrors: every write goes to both disks, reads can use either, and one disk may fail. Capacity is half the raw total. RAID 5 uses N disks, stores N − 1 disks' worth of data and one disk's worth of parity spread across the array; one disk may fail. RAID 6 adds a second, independent parity (Reed-Solomon codes), stores N − 2 disks' worth, and survives any two failures. RAID 10 (1+0) stripes across mirrored pairs: half the capacity, and it survives at least one failure and up to one per mirror pair, but two failures in the same pair destroy data. (Levels 2, 3 and 4 exist but are rarely used.)

The small-write problem

Parity makes small writes expensive. To change one block of a RAID 5 stripe, the controller does not need to read the whole stripe. Because parity is XOR, the new parity can be computed from the old data, the old parity, and the new data:

P_new = P_old XOR D_old XOR D_new

That takes two reads (old data, old parity) and two writes (new data, new parity): four disk I/Os for one logical write, the small-write penalty. RAID 6 must update two parities: six I/Os. RAID 1 and 10 write two copies: two I/Os. The next program shows the parity algebra on one byte and prints the RAID 5 layout of Figure 11.4.

#include <stdio.h>

static void bits(const char *label, unsigned char v) {
    printf("%-22s ", label);
    for (int i = 7; i >= 0; i--) putchar((v >> i) & 1 ? '1' : '0');
    printf("  (0x%02X)\n", v);
}

int main(void) {
    /* One byte from each of three data disks, plus parity on a fourth. */
    unsigned char d0 = 0xB2, d1 = 0x5C, d2 = 0x0F;
    unsigned char p = d0 ^ d1 ^ d2;
    bits("D0", d0); bits("D1", d1); bits("D2", d2); bits("P = D0^D1^D2", p);

    unsigned char rebuilt = d0 ^ d2 ^ p;                 /* disk 1 died */
    bits("rebuild D1 = D0^D2^P", rebuilt);
    printf("rebuilt matches D1: %s\n\n", rebuilt == d1 ? "yes" : "NO");

    /* Small write: change D2 without reading D0 and D1. */
    unsigned char new_d2 = 0xF0;
    unsigned char new_p  = p ^ d2 ^ new_d2;              /* read old D2 and old P: 2 reads */
    bits("new D2", new_d2);
    bits("P' = P^D2^D2'", new_p);
    printf("equals full recompute: %s  (2 reads + 2 writes = 4 I/Os)\n\n",
           new_p == (d0 ^ d1 ^ new_d2) ? "yes" : "NO");

    /* Left-asymmetric RAID 5 layout, 4 disks: parity starts on the last disk and rotates left. */
    printf("RAID 5 layout (left-asymmetric), 4 disks (P = parity, Dn = data block n)\n");
    printf("stripe  disk0 disk1 disk2 disk3\n");
    int next = 0;
    for (int s = 0; s < 4; s++) {
        int pd = 3 - (s % 4);
        printf("%4d   ", s);
        for (int d = 0; d < 4; d++) {
            if (d == pd) printf("  P   ");
            else printf(" D%-3d ", next++);
        }
        printf("\n");
    }
    return 0;
}

If one disk fails, every lost block is recomputed as the XOR of the surviving blocks of its stripe (D1 = D0 XOR D2 XOR P), as the "rebuild" line shows. Full-stripe writes avoid the penalty, because the new parity is computed from the new data alone with no reads, which is why large sequential writes on RAID 5 are fast. Controllers use a write-back cache to turn small writes into full-stripe ones.

Rebuilds and the limits of RAID

When a disk fails, the array runs degraded (reads of the lost disk's data need reconstruction) until a hot spare is rebuilt. Rebuild time is at least the capacity divided by the write speed: a 4 TB disk at 100 MB/s needs 4 × 10¹² / 10⁸ = 40,000 s, about 11.1 hours, and longer under load. During that time the array is vulnerable. With RAID 5 the rebuild must read every bit of all surviving disks, and any unrecoverable read error (URE) then loses data. A consumer drive is specified at one URE per 10¹⁴ bits read. Rebuilding our six-disk RAID 5 reads 5 × 4 TB = 20 TB = 1.6 × 10¹⁴ bits, so the expected number of UREs is 1.6 and the chance of at least one is 1 − e^(−1.6), about 80 percent. Enterprise drives (1 in 10¹⁵) cut that to about 15 percent. This is the main argument for RAID 6 or RAID 10 with large drives.

RAID is not a backup

RAID protects against disk failure only. It does nothing about accidental deletion, corruption, ransomware, a failed controller or a fire; it faithfully mirrors a deleted file. A write hole can also corrupt RAID 5 if power fails between the data and the parity write, so controllers use battery-backed caches and Linux md can use a journal. Keep separate backups.

Linux provides software RAID through md (managed by mdadm) and logical volumes through LVM; ZFS and Btrfs integrate RAID-like redundancy with the file system, which lets them detect and repair corruption using checksums.

Disk management

A new disk is prepared in stages. Low-level formatting (done at the factory) writes sector headers and spare sectors. The OS then partitions the disk (a partition table in MBR or GPT format divides it into regions, each treated as a separate logical disk) and creates a file system in each partition (logical formatting: the superblock, inode tables and free-space structures of Week 12). On boot, firmware (BIOS or UEFI) loads a small boot loader from a known location, which loads the kernel. Drives remap bad blocks to spares transparently; the OS may also keep its own bad-block list. Swap space, used for the paging of Weeks 9 and 10, can be a raw partition or a file; raw partitions avoid file-system overhead, while swap files are easier to resize. On SSDs, swapping consumes write endurance, so Linux can compress pages in memory first (zswap or zram).

Exercises

Exercise 11.1warm-up

Access time of a fast disk

A 10,000 RPM disk has an average seek of 5 ms and transfers at 200 MB/s. (a) Find the average rotational latency. (b) Find the average time and IOPS for a random 4 KiB read. (c) Find the same for a random 1 MiB read and the resulting throughput in MB/s. (d) Which delay dominates in each case?

Show solution

(a) One rotation takes 60 / 10,000 = 6 ms; the average latency is half, 3 ms.

(b) Transfer of 4096 bytes at 200 MB/s: 4096 / 200,000,000 = 0.0205 ms. Total 5 + 3 + 0.0205 = 8.02 ms, so about 1000 / 8.02 = 124.7 IOPS.

(c) Transfer of 1,048,576 bytes: 1,048,576 / 200,000,000 = 5.243 ms. Total 5 + 3 + 5.243 = 13.24 ms, which is 75.5 IOPS and 75.5 × 1.048576 ≈ 79.2 MB/s.

(d) For 4 KiB, the seek (5 ms) and rotation (3 ms) take 99.7 percent of the time. For 1 MiB, transfer takes 5.24 of 13.24 ms, about 40 percent: larger transfers amortize the positioning delay but throughput (79 MB/s) is still below the 200 MB/s streaming rate.

Exercise 11.2core

Disk scheduling trace

The head is at cylinder 100 on a disk with cylinders 0 to 199 and is moving toward 199. The queue, in arrival order, is 55, 58, 39, 18, 90, 160, 150, 38, 184. Compute the total head movement for (a) FCFS, (b) SSTF, (c) LOOK, (d) C-LOOK counting the return jump. (e) Why might SSTF be a poor choice on a busy server?

Show solution

(a) FCFS: 100 → 55 → 58 → 39 → 18 → 90 → 160 → 150 → 38 → 184 moves 45, 3, 19, 21, 72, 70, 10, 112, 146: total 498.

(b) SSTF order: 90 (10), 58 (32), 55 (3), 39 (16), 38 (1), 18 (20), 150 (132), 160 (10), 184 (24). Total 10 + 32 + 3 + 16 + 1 + 20 + 132 + 10 + 24 = 248.

(c) LOOK toward 199: serve 150, 160, 184 (moves 50, 10, 24 = 84), reverse and serve 90, 58, 55, 39, 38, 18 (from 184 down to 18 = 166). Total 84 + 166 = 250.

(d) C-LOOK: 100 → 184 (84), jump 184 → 18 (166), then 18 → 90 (72). Total 84 + 166 + 72 = 322.

(e) SSTF always prefers the nearest request. If requests near the head keep arriving, a request at a far cylinder (say 190 when the head hovers around 20) can wait indefinitely: starvation. SCAN-family algorithms bound the wait to roughly one or two sweeps.

Exercise 11.3core

RAID capacity and write throughput

A server has eight 2 TB disks, each capable of 100 random IOPS. For RAID 0, RAID 5, RAID 6 and RAID 10 give (a) usable capacity, (b) number of disk failures always survived, and (c) maximum sustained random small-write IOPS.

Show solution

Raw capacity is 16 TB and the raw IOPS are 8 × 100 = 800.

Level (a) Usable (b) Always survives (c) Small-write IOPS
RAID 0 16 TB 0 800 / 1 = 800
RAID 5 14 TB (7 × 2) 1 800 / 4 = 200
RAID 6 12 TB (6 × 2) 2 800 / 6 ≈ 133
RAID 10 8 TB (4 × 2) 1 (up to 4 if one per pair) 800 / 2 = 400

RAID 10 gives twice the small-write rate of RAID 5 for 6 TB less capacity. RAID 6 gives the strongest protection, at one third the write rate of RAID 0.

Exercise 11.4core

XOR parity

A RAID 5 stripe holds three data bytes D0 = 11001010, D1 = 01010110 and D2 = 00111100 and a parity byte P. (a) Compute P. (b) Disk 1 fails. Rebuild D1. (c) The host now overwrites D1 with 10011101 (without reading D0 or D2). Compute the new parity and count the disk I/Os.

Show solution

(a) D0 XOR D1 = 11001010 XOR 01010110 = 10011100; XOR D2 (00111100) = 10100000. So P = 10100000 (0xA0).

(b) D1 = D0 XOR D2 XOR P: 11001010 XOR 00111100 = 11110110; XOR 10100000 = 01010110, which equals the original D1.

(c) P_new = P_old XOR D1_old XOR D1_new: 10100000 XOR 01010110 = 11110110; XOR 10011101 = 01101011. P_new = 01101011. Check: D0 XOR D1_new XOR D2 = 11001010 XOR 10011101 XOR 00111100 = 01101011. ✓. The update uses 2 reads (old D1, old P) and 2 writes (new D1, new P): 4 disk I/Os.

Exercise 11.5core

Write amplification and SSD lifetime

(a) A garbage-collection victim block has 256 pages, of which 192 are still valid. Compute the write amplification for the writes this collection makes possible. (b) A 1 TB TLC drive endures 3,000 program/erase cycles per cell and the workload runs at WA = 4. If the host writes 100 GB a day, how many years until the endurance is used up? (c) Name two things that lower WA.

Show solution

(a) Collecting the block copies 192 valid pages (192 extra flash writes) and frees 64 pages, which the host can then fill with 64 new pages. Flash writes = 192 + 64 = 256 for 64 host writes: WA = 256 / 64 = 4.

(b) Total flash writes allowed ≈ 1 TB × 3,000 = 3,000 TB. Host writes absorbed = 3,000 / 4 = 750 TB. At 0.1 TB per day, that is 750 / 0.1 = 7,500 days, about 20.5 years. (Real drives also have other limits, but the arithmetic shows why WA matters.)

(c) Any two of: more over-provisioning, TRIM, sequential or large writes (which invalidate whole blocks together), separating hot and cold data, and keeping the drive less full.

Exercise 11.6core

Mapping a byte to a disk in RAID 0

A RAID 0 array has 4 disks and a stripe unit of 64 KiB (65,536 bytes). Stripe units are placed round-robin: unit 0 on disk 0, unit 1 on disk 1, and so on, wrapping after disk 3. Which disk holds byte offset 1,000,000 of the array, at what byte offset on that disk, and which stripe row is it in?

Show solution

Unit number = ⌊1,000,000 / 65,536⌋ = 15 (since 15 × 65,536 = 983,040). Offset within the unit = 1,000,000 − 983,040 = 16,960.

Disk = 15 mod 4 = 3. Stripe row = ⌊15 / 4⌋ = 3. Offset on disk 3 = 3 × 65,536 + 16,960 = 213,568.

Check: row 3 on disk 3 is stripe unit 3 × 4 + 3 = 15 ✓.

Exercise 11.7challenging

Rebuild time and unrecoverable errors

A RAID 5 array has five 8 TB disks. A disk fails and is replaced by a hot spare. (a) How many bytes and bits must be read to rebuild it? (b) With a URE rate of one per 10¹⁵ bits, find the expected number of UREs and the probability of at least one (treat errors as a Poisson process). (c) If the spare is written at 150 MB/s and the rebuild is not throttled, how long does it take? (d) How does RAID 6 change the picture?

Show solution

(a) Four surviving disks must be read in full: 4 × 8 TB = 32 TB = 3.2 × 10¹³ bytes = 2.56 × 10¹⁴ bits.

(b) Expected UREs λ = 2.56 × 10¹⁴ / 10¹⁵ = 0.256. P(at least one) = 1 − e^(−0.256) ≈ 0.226, about 23 percent (with consumer-grade 10¹⁴, λ = 2.56 and the probability is 1 − e^(−2.56) ≈ 92.3 percent).

(c) The new disk is written in full: 8 × 10¹² / 1.5 × 10⁸ = 53,333 s ≈ 14.8 hours, and in practice longer because the array also serves users.

(d) With RAID 6, after one disk fails the stripes still have one redundant parity left, so a URE found during the rebuild can be corrected from it, and a second whole-disk failure during the long rebuild window is also survivable. The cost is N − 2 instead of N − 1 usable disks and six I/Os per small write.

Part 2: the disk scheduler

The program below reads an optional head position and request list from the command line (./sched 100 55 58 39 18 90 160 150 38 184); with no arguments it uses the chapter's queue. SCAN, C-SCAN, LOOK and C-LOOK are all one sweep routine with two switches: whether to travel to the physical edge, and whether to wrap around or reverse.

#include <stdio.h>
#include <stdlib.h>
#include <string.h>

#define MAXQ 64
static int q[MAXQ], n, head = 53, maxcyl = 199;

static int cmp(const void *a, const void *b) { return *(const int *)a - *(const int *)b; }
static int absd(int x) { return x < 0 ? -x : x; }

static void show(const char *name, const int *path, int len, int total) {
    printf("%-16s %3d  ", name, total);
    for (int i = 0; i < len; i++) printf("%d%s", path[i], i + 1 < len ? " " : "");
    printf("\n");
}

static void fcfs(void) {
    int path[MAXQ + 2], len = 0, pos = head, total = 0;
    path[len++] = pos;
    for (int i = 0; i < n; i++) { total += absd(q[i] - pos); pos = q[i]; path[len++] = pos; }
    show("FCFS", path, len, total);
}

static void sstf(void) {
    int path[MAXQ + 2], len = 0, pos = head, total = 0, done[MAXQ] = {0};
    path[len++] = pos;
    for (int k = 0; k < n; k++) {
        int best = -1;
        for (int i = 0; i < n; i++)
            if (!done[i] && (best < 0 || absd(q[i] - pos) < absd(q[best] - pos))) best = i;
        done[best] = 1; total += absd(q[best] - pos); pos = q[best]; path[len++] = pos;
    }
    show("SSTF", path, len, total);
}

/* kind: 0 = SCAN, 1 = C-SCAN, 2 = LOOK, 3 = C-LOOK; up = 1 means toward high cylinders */
static void sweep(const char *name, int kind, int up) {
    int s[MAXQ], path[MAXQ + 4], len = 0, pos = head, total = 0;
    memcpy(s, q, n * sizeof(int));
    qsort(s, n, sizeof(int), cmp);
    int lo = 0;
    while (lo < n && s[lo] < head) lo++;               /* s[lo..n-1] are >= head */
    path[len++] = pos;
    int edge = (kind == 0 || kind == 1);               /* go all the way to the edge */
    int wrap = (kind == 1 || kind == 3);               /* circular: jump back, same direction */
    if (up) {
        for (int i = lo; i < n; i++) { total += s[i] - pos; pos = s[i]; path[len++] = pos; }
        if (edge && pos != maxcyl) { total += maxcyl - pos; pos = maxcyl; path[len++] = pos; }
        if (wrap) {
            if (lo > 0) {
                int first = edge ? 0 : s[0];
                total += absd(pos - first); pos = first; path[len++] = pos;   /* the return jump counts */
                for (int i = 0; i < lo; i++) if (s[i] != pos) { total += s[i] - pos; pos = s[i]; path[len++] = pos; }
            }
        } else {
            for (int i = lo - 1; i >= 0; i--) { total += pos - s[i]; pos = s[i]; path[len++] = pos; }
        }
    } else {
        for (int i = lo - 1; i >= 0; i--) { total += pos - s[i]; pos = s[i]; path[len++] = pos; }
        if (edge && pos != 0) { total += pos; pos = 0; path[len++] = pos; }
        for (int i = lo; i < n; i++) { total += s[i] - pos; pos = s[i]; path[len++] = pos; }
    }
    show(name, path, len, total);
}

int main(int argc, char **argv) {
    if (argc > 2) {                                     /* sched HEAD q1 q2 ... */
        head = atoi(argv[1]);
        for (int i = 2; i < argc && n < MAXQ; i++) q[n++] = atoi(argv[i]);
    } else {
        int d[] = {98, 183, 37, 122, 14, 124, 65, 67};
        n = sizeof d / sizeof d[0]; memcpy(q, d, sizeof d);
    }
    printf("head at %d, cylinders 0..%d, %d requests\n", head, maxcyl, n);
    printf("%-16s %3s  %s\n", "algorithm", "mv", "service order");
    fcfs();
    sstf();
    sweep("SCAN  (to 0)", 0, 0);
    sweep("SCAN  (to 199)", 0, 1);
    sweep("C-SCAN (to 199)", 1, 1);
    sweep("LOOK  (to 199)", 2, 1);
    sweep("C-LOOK (to 199)", 3, 1);
    return 0;
}

Common mistakes and debugging tips

Symptom Likely cause What to do
Average rotational latency twice as big as the textbook Used a full rotation instead of half Latency = (60 / RPM) / 2
SCAN total differs from the classic answer Went to the wrong end first, or forgot that SCAN reaches the edge (cylinder 0 or 199) Draw the path; include the trip to the edge for SCAN and C-SCAN, not for LOOK
C-SCAN total 183 instead of 382 Left the return jump out of the count State your convention; this course counts the jump
Head movement counted as sum of positions Added cylinder numbers instead of differences Sum the absolute differences between consecutive stops
RAID 5 capacity wrong Used N instead of N − 1 One disk's worth of space holds parity
RAID 10 "survives 3 failures" for six disks Ignored which disks fail Guaranteed only 1; more only if no two failures hit the same mirror pair
Write IOPS equal to read IOPS on RAID 5 Forgot the small-write penalty Divide raw IOPS by 4 (RAID 5), 6 (RAID 6) or 2 (RAID 1 and 10)
Parity update computed from the whole stripe Missed the shortcut P_new = P_old XOR D_old XOR D_new
SSD slows down when nearly full Garbage collection has little room to work, so WA grows Leave free space, enable TRIM, use over-provisioning

Key takeaways

Key takeaways

  • HDD access time is seek plus rotational latency plus transfer; for small random I/O positioning is over 99 percent of the time, so sequential access is hundreds of times faster than random.
  • Flash cannot be overwritten in place; the FTL maps logical to physical pages, collects garbage, levels wear and causes write amplification, and TRIM and over-provisioning keep it low.
  • For the queue 98, 183, 37, 122, 14, 124, 65, 67 at head 53: FCFS 640, SSTF 236, SCAN 236 (toward 0) or 331 (toward 199), C-SCAN 382, LOOK 299, C-LOOK 322 cylinders.
  • SSTF can starve requests; SCAN-family algorithms bound waiting; on modern drives and SSDs the real ordering is done by firmware, and the OS mostly merges and enforces deadlines.
  • RAID 0 stripes, RAID 1 mirrors, RAID 5 stripes with one parity (N − 1 usable), RAID 6 with two (N − 2), RAID 10 stripes mirrors (half usable).
  • A small write on RAID 5 costs 4 I/Os (RAID 6: 6, RAID 1 and 10: 2); parity is XOR, so any lost block equals the XOR of the others.
  • RAID is not a backup, and long rebuilds of large disks expose RAID 5 to unrecoverable read errors.
